--- SLIDE 2: PROBLEM (Logan) ---
Thanks, Josh. Our goal is to predict whether a credit card transaction is
fraud, based on things like the amount, category, time, and location. A
big signal is where the purchase happened. If it's far from the
cardholder's home, or just out of pattern for that person, it looks
suspicious. The challenge is that fraud is a tiny fraction of all
transactions. On this slide, one dot in a hundred is fraud, and in our
generated data it's about 1.2 in a hundred, so the model has to find a few
needles in a very large haystack. Josh will tell you where our data comes
from.

--- SLIDE 3: DATA (Josh) ---
For data, we're using the Sparkov Data Generation tool, which is public on
GitHub. It simulates credit card transactions, including fraud. It gives
us customers with a home location, merchants with a category and location,
amounts, timestamps, and a fraud label. Since it's simulated, there's no
private or personal data involved, and we can generate as much as we need.
Logan will explain what we do with it.
Source: https://github.com/namebrandon/Sparkov_Data_Generation. Confirm
exact column names after generating the CSVs.

--- SLIDE 4: FEATURES (Logan) ---
Raw columns only get us so far, so we'll build features that capture
normal card behavior. For location, we'll calculate the distance from the
customer's home to the merchant. For time, we'll use hour of day, weekend,
and customer age. For behavior, we'll compare each amount to the
customer's average and count how many transactions happened in the past
hour and day. And for movement, we'll look at how much time and distance
has passed since their previous transaction. All of these only use past
transactions, so nothing from the future leaks into training. Josh will
cover the models.

--- SLIDE 5: METHODS (Josh) ---
Thanks, Logan. We're comparing four models, from simple to powerful.
Logistic regression is our baseline. It learns a weight for each feature
and outputs a fraud probability. A decision tree is a single flowchart of
yes/no questions, which is easy to read but can overfit. A random forest
combines hundreds of trees that vote, which captures non-linear patterns
and shows which features matter most. And gradient boosting builds trees
one after another, each fixing the last one's mistakes, and it's often the
strongest on tables like ours. To deal with the imbalance, we'll use class
weights, so every model treats a missed fraud as more costly than a false
alarm. We'll build everything in Python with scikit-learn and XGBoost or
LightGBM. Logan will explain how we measure the results.

--- SLIDE 6: METRICS (Logan) ---
We can't use accuracy here. Fraud is only about 1.2% of our transactions,
so a model that says "not fraud" every single time is 98.8% accurate and
catches nothing. So we'll use precision, which is how many flagged
transactions are really fraud, and recall, which is how much of the real
fraud we catch. F1 combines the two, and that will be our main number for
comparing models. Josh will walk through the plan.

--- SLIDE 7: PLAN (Josh) ---
Here's the plan from start to finish. First, we generate the data and load

transactions and testing on later ones, like a real fraud system would.
Fifth, we train and compare all four models and tune them on a validation
set. And last, we evaluate with our metrics and run a demo on hand-built
transactions.

--- SLIDE 9: EXPECTED OUTCOMES (Josh) ---

PART 2: QUESTIONS YOU MAY GET, WITH ANSWERS
==================================================

BASICS

Q: What problem are you solving?
A: Flagging fraudulent credit card transactions automatically, using the
   amount, merchant category, time, and especially the location of each
   purchase compared to where the cardholder lives.

Q: Is this deep learning?
A: No. We're using logistic regression, a decision tree, a random
   forest, and gradient boosting, which are classical machine learning
   methods.

Q: Why these four models?
A: They go from simple to powerful. Logistic regression is a simple,
   explainable baseline. A decision tree is a single readable flowchart.
   Random forest combines many trees to capture non-linear patterns and
   reports feature importance. Gradient boosting builds trees in
   sequence to fix earlier mistakes and is often the strongest on tabular
   data. Comparing them shows how much extra complexity helps.

Q: Why not KNN or SVM?
A: They get slow on a dataset with millions of rows, and tree-based
   models usually do better on tabular data like this.

Q: Why did you choose fraud detection?
A: It's a real-world problem with a clear label, location and behavior
   features that let us do real feature engineering, and a built-in
   challenge (extreme class imbalance) that makes the modeling
   interesting.

DATA

Q: Where does the data come from?
A: The Sparkov Data Generation tool, which is public on GitHub. We run it
   ourselves to produce customers, merchants, and transactions with a
   fraud label. It's not from Kaggle.

Q: Why simulated data instead of real data?
A: Real transaction data is private. Public real datasets are mostly
   anonymized (the columns are scrambled), so they have no location
   information, and our distance feature wouldn't be possible. The
   generator gives us locations, categories, and timestamps.

Q: Does it include private or personal data?
A: No. Everything is simulated and publicly reproducible.

Q: How much fraud is in the data?
A: In our generated data, fraud is about 1.2% of transactions (9,500 of
   822,484).

FEATURES AND PREPROCESSING

Q: What is the haversine formula?
A: A formula that calculates the distance between two points on a sphere
   from their latitude and longitude. We use it to measure how far each
   merchant is from the customer's home.

Q: Why drop name, street, and card number?
A: They're identifiers, not behavior. They have no predictive value for
   new customers and could let the model memorize individual customers
   instead of learning fraud patterns.

Q: Why one-hot encode categories?
A: Models need numbers. One-hot encoding turns a category like "gas" into
   its own 0/1 column without implying an order between categories.

Q: Why scale the inputs?
A: Logistic regression is sensitive to feature scale (amount in dollars
   versus hour of day). Random forests don't need scaling.

Q: What is data leakage and how do you avoid it?
A: Leakage is when the model gets information it wouldn't have in real
   life, like using future transactions to describe a current one. We
   avoid it by building customer history features from past transactions
   only and by splitting train and test data by time.

Q: Why split by time instead of randomly?
A: A real fraud system trains on the past and predicts the future. A
   random split would mix future transactions into training and make
   results look better than they should.

METRICS AND IMBALANCE

Q: Why not just use accuracy?
A: Fraud is about 1.2% of our
   transactions, so predicting "not fraud" every time scores 98.8%
   accuracy while catching zero fraud.

Q: What are precision and recall?
A: Precision is, of the transactions we flag, how many are really fraud.
   Recall is, of all the real fraud, how much we catch.

Q: Which matters more, precision or recall?
A: It's a tradeoff. Low recall means missed fraud and lost money. Low
   precision means false alarms that annoy customers. F1 balances the
   two, so it's our main comparison number, and we'll report both
   separately too.

Q: How are you handling class imbalance?
A: Class weights, which tell every model a missed fraud costs more than
   a false alarm. If time allows, we could also try SMOTE, which creates
   synthetic fraud examples.

Q: What is a class weight?
A: A multiplier on the penalty for getting a class wrong. Giving fraud a
   large weight pushes the model to pay attention to the rare class.

MODELING

Q: How will you tune the models?
A: On a validation set held out from the training data, adjusting things
   like tree depth and the number of trees for the tree-based models. The
   test set is only used for the final results.

Q: What do you expect the results to look like?
A: We expect the random forest and gradient boosting to beat the logistic
   regression baseline on recall and F1, and the distance and behavior features to rank high
   in feature importance. We'll report what we actually find, even if it
   differs.

Q: What if the model scores near-perfect?
A: That can happen with simulated data, where fraud patterns may be
   simpler than in reality. We'd report it honestly and discuss it as a
   limitation.

Q: Where is the effort in this project?
A: Feature engineering (distance and customer-history features),
   preprocessing, a time-based split, imbalance handling, comparing four
   models with the right metrics, and a demo. The library calls are the
   small part.

DEMO AND LIMITATIONS

Q: How will the demo work?
A: We'll hand-build 10-20 example transactions, some normal and near
   home, some far away or unusually large, and run them through the
   trained model to show the fraud probability for each. No real bank
   data is used.

Q: What are the limitations?
A: The data is simulated, so results may not transfer directly to
   real-world spending. Distance is measured from a single home
   location. And hand-built demo transactions aren't a real evaluation.

Q: Who has done something similar?
A: sharmasapna on GitHub classified fraud on Sparkov data with about 1.3
   million transactions. Our focus is the distance-from-home feature,
   per-customer behavior features, a time-based split, a four-model
   comparison, and a scoring demo.

Q: What would you do with more time?
A: Try SMOTE, run a wider hyperparameter search, and report PR-AUC to
   summarize performance across all possible cutoffs.

Q: What is your timeline?
A: Proposal Oct 20 and 22. Intermediate Nov 10 and 12, with data
   generated, features built, baseline results, and limitations. Final
   presentation Nov 24 and 26 with all four models tuned and the
   demo working. Final report and GitHub code by Dec 6.

PART 3: DEFINITIONS (QUICK GLOSSARY)
==================================================

PROJECT BASICS
Supervised learning: Training a model on examples that already have the
  right answer (here, each transaction is labeled fraud or not fraud).
Classification: Predicting a category (fraud or not fraud) instead of a
  number.
Label: The right answer for a transaction (fraud or not fraud).
Feature: One input the model uses, like amount, hour of day, or distance
  from home.
Feature engineering: Creating new, more useful features from the raw
  columns, like turning two sets of lat/long into a distance.
Baseline: A simple model used as the point of comparison. Fancier models
  have to beat it to be worth the extra complexity.
Classical machine learning: Methods like logistic regression and random
  forests, as opposed to deep learning (neural networks).

DATA
Sparkov: The public tool we use to generate simulated credit card
  transactions, including fraud.
Simulated (synthetic) data: Data created by a program to look like real
  data, with no real people involved.
Anonymized data: Real data with the columns scrambled to hide identities.
  It protects privacy but removes useful details like location.
Identifier: A column that names a specific person or card (name, street,
  card number). Not useful for predicting fraud.
Latitude / longitude: Coordinates that mark a location on Earth.

PREPROCESSING
Preprocessing: Cleaning and converting raw data into a form models can
  use.
Parsing timestamps: Converting date-and-time text into values the code
  understands, so we can pull out hour, weekday, and so on.
One-hot encoding: Turning a category like "gas" into its own 0/1 column.
Scaling: Putting features on similar ranges so none dominates just
  because its numbers are bigger. Needed for logistic regression, not for
  random forests.
Haversine formula: Calculates the distance between two points on a sphere
  from their latitude and longitude.

MODELS
Logistic regression: A model that learns a weight for each feature and
  outputs a probability between 0 and 1.
Decision tree: A flowchart of yes/no questions (for example, "is the
  distance over 200 miles?") that ends in a prediction.
Random forest: Many decision trees, each trained on a random slice of
  the data, that vote on the answer.
Gradient boosting: Trees built one after another, each one correcting the
  mistakes of the previous ones. XGBoost and LightGBM are popular
  versions.
Overfitting (see Training Setup): a single decision tree is especially
  prone to it, which is why we also use ensembles.
Feature importance: A score showing how much each feature helped the
  model. Random forests report it automatically.
Probability / threshold: The model outputs a fraud probability, and a
  threshold (like 0.5) decides when to flag a transaction. Changing the
  threshold trades precision against recall.
Hyperparameter: A setting you choose before training, like the number of
  trees or how deep they can grow.
scikit-learn: The Python library that provides logistic regression,
  random forests, and evaluation tools.
pandas: The Python library for loading and working with tables of data.

IMBALANCE
Class imbalance: When one class is far rarer than the other. Fraud is a
  tiny fraction of transactions.
Class weights: A multiplier that makes mistakes on the rare class (fraud)
  count more during training.
SMOTE: A technique that creates synthetic examples of the rare class to
  balance the training data. (Optional extension for us.)

EVALUATION
Confusion matrix: A table of results with four counts:
  - True positive (TP): fraud we correctly flagged.
  - False positive (FP): normal transaction we wrongly flagged.
  - False negative (FN): fraud we missed.
  - True negative (TN): normal transaction correctly left alone.
Accuracy: The share of all predictions that were right. Misleading when
  one class is very rare.
Precision: TP / (TP + FP). Of the transactions we flag, how many are
  really fraud.
Recall: TP / (TP + FN). Of all real fraud, how much we catch.
F1 score: One number that balances precision and recall. It's high only
  when both are high.
PR-AUC: The area under the precision-recall curve. It summarizes
  performance across all possible thresholds and is a standard metric for
  imbalanced data. (Optional extra metric for us.)

TRAINING SETUP
Training set: The data the model learns from.
Validation set: Data held out from training, used to tune settings.
Test set: Data used only once at the end to report final results.
Time-based split: Training on earlier transactions and testing on later
  ones, like a real fraud system would.
Data leakage: When the model gets information it wouldn't have in real
  life, such as future transactions, making results look better than
  they are.
Overfitting: When a model memorizes the training data and does poorly on
  new data.

Here is our checklist against the proposal. We generated 822K transactions

--- SLIDE 4: DATA (Logan) ---
The data has 822,484 transactions from 999 customers, with no duplicates
or missing values. Fraud is 1.2% of transactions, about 9,500 cases. That
is slightly above the "under one percent" we estimated in the proposal,
and we are correcting that figure here. A model that always answers "not
fraud" would be 98.8% accurate and catch nothing, which is why we do not
rely on accuracy. Josh will describe the features we built.

--- SLIDE 5: FEATURES (Josh) ---
We built 31 model inputs in four groups. Base features are amount,
customer age, and city size, plus fourteen merchant category columns. Time
features are hour, weekday, weekend, and night. Distance is the haversine
distance from home to the merchant. History features describe each
customer's past, such as the amount compared with their average and
purchases in the last hour. These use only earlier transactions, so
nothing leaks from the future. Logan will explain how we reduced the
dimensionality of these inputs.

--- SLIDE 6: DIMENSIONALITY REDUCTION: PCA (Logan) ---
With 31 inputs that overlap, we applied principal component analysis, or
PCA, which rotates the data into new directions ordered by how much
variation they carry. It takes 25 components to keep 95 percent of the
variance, and the first two keep 17 percent. The first component mostly
reflects recent activity, such as purchases in the last day, and the
second mostly reflects amount. In the two-dimensional view, fraud overlaps
normal activity heavily, so no simple projection separates it. Josh will
show a nonlinear view and the effect of reduced inputs on the models.

--- SLIDE 7: T-SNE AND PCA IN THE MODELS (Josh) ---
To look for structure that PCA cannot show, we used t-SNE, a nonlinear
method, on a balanced sample of 3,000 fraud and 3,000 normal transactions.
Fraud forms local clusters: on average 84 percent of a fraud point's ten
nearest neighbors are also fraud. We then trained the models on reduced
inputs. PCA on only the 13 continuous features keeps most of the
performance, with the random forest at 0.70 versus 0.76. PCA on all 31
features drops it to 0.004, probably because the fraud signal sits in
flags and categories that the rotation mixes. We have not tested that
explanation. Logan will explain how we validated the models.

--- SLIDE 8: TIME-AWARE CROSS-VALIDATION (Logan) ---
We validated with time-aware cross-validation. The January to November
development period is cut into six chunks. In each of five folds, the
models train on the earlier chunks and validate on the next one, so they
never see the future. We chose each model's settings by average PR-AUC
across the folds and its decision threshold from the pooled validation
predictions. Each model was then refit on the whole development period and
tested once on December. The folds also show instability: the random
forest averages 0.73 with a spread of 0.10, while gradient boosting's
spread is 0.19. Josh will present the early results.

--- SLIDE 9: EARLY MODEL RESULTS (Josh) ---
Here are the four models on the held-out December test, beside their
cross-validated PR-AUC. Logistic regression struggles, with an F1 of 0.04,
because the fraud pattern is non-linear. The decision tree reaches 0.66,
the random forest 0.72, and gradient boosting 0.30. Gradient boosting's
low F1 comes from a threshold that was too strict after refitting, so its
PR-AUC of 0.66 is the fairer figure. The random forest is best on both
test and cross-validation. These are first-pass results with small tuning
grids. Logan will cover the limitations.

--- SLIDE 10: LIMITATIONS (Logan) ---
We want to be clear about the limits. The data is simulated, so results
may not transfer to real spending. Distance from home had no signal: its
single-feature score is 0.50, a coin flip. Fraud also drifts, from 1.3% of
transactions in January to November to 0.5% in the test month, and cross-
validation scores vary by fold. Finally, tuning is limited to small grids
and thresholds do not always transfer, so the numbers should improve. Josh
will cover our scope and what has changed.

--- SLIDE 11: SCOPE AND CHANGES SINCE PROPOSAL (Josh) ---
Our title and topic are unchanged: detecting credit card fraud with

PART 2: QUESTIONS YOU MAY GET, WITH ANSWERS
==================================================

Q: Did your topic or title change?
A: No. It is still credit card fraud detection. Since the proposal we
   added two models (four total), time-aware cross-validation, a time-
   based split, PCA and t-SNE, and we generate the data with the public
   Sparkov tool.

Q: How much fraud is in the data?
A: 9,500 of 822,484 transactions, about 1.2%. Our proposal estimated under
   1%; we corrected it after measuring.

Q: What loss function do you use?
A: Every model optimizes cross-entropy. Logistic regression minimizes
   binary cross-entropy (log loss). Gradient boosting uses XGBoost's
   binary:logistic objective, which is also log loss. The decision tree
   and random forest choose splits with the entropy criterion (information
   gain, which is built from cross-entropy), instead of the default Gini
   impurity.

Q: How did you use cross-validation?
A: Time-aware, expanding-window cross-validation with 5 folds over January
   to November. Each fold trains on earlier chunks and validates on the
   next chunk, so the models never train on the future. Settings were
   chosen by mean PR-AUC across folds, and December was held out until the
   end. Random forest averaged 0.73 ± 0.10.

Q: Why not ordinary shuffled k-fold?
A: Shuffling mixes future transactions into training and inflates scores,
   because history features and fraud patterns depend on time. Time-
   ordered folds match how a real system is used.

Q: What dimensionality reduction did you do?
A: PCA on the 31 standardized inputs (it takes 25 components to keep 95%
   of the variance), a t-SNE view of a balanced sample, and models trained
   on reduced inputs. PCA on only the 13 continuous features kept most
   performance (random forest 0.70 vs 0.76); PCA on all features collapsed
   it (0.004).

Q: Why did PCA on all features hurt so much?
A: We observed it but did not isolate the cause. A likely reason is that
   the fraud signal lives in flags and categories (such as the night flag)
   that a rotation across all features mixes together. We treat that as a
   hypothesis.

Q: Which model is best?
A: The random forest: test PR-AUC 0.76, F1 0.72, and the most stable
   cross-validation (0.73 ± 0.10).

Q: Why is gradient boosting's F1 so low?
A: Its PR-AUC is 0.66, but the decision threshold chosen in cross-
   validation (0.99) was too strict after the final refit, so it flags
   only 99 fraud cases (F1 0.30). PR-AUC does not depend on a threshold,
   so it is the fairer comparison.

Q: Why is logistic regression so weak?
A: PR-AUC 0.006. Fraud depends on combinations of amount, hour, and
   customer history, which a straight-line model cannot express. It
   remains a useful baseline.

Q: Why didn't distance from home help?
A: In this generated data, fraud is no farther from home than normal
   purchases (medians 49.0 vs 48.9 miles; single-feature score 0.50). It
   is a finding about the simulator, not about real fraud.

Q: How do you avoid leakage?
A: History features use only a customer's earlier transactions, folds are
   time-ordered, and December was evaluated once.

Q: Why does the test fraud rate differ?
A: Fraud is 1.3% of transactions in January to November but 0.5% in
   December. The generator does not spread fraud evenly across months. We
   list it as a limitation.

Q: Is any of this real data?
A: No. It is simulated with the public Sparkov generator. No private or
   personal data is used.

Q: Who did what?
A: Josh: logistic regression, decision tree, feature pipeline, ablation.
   Logan: random forest, gradient boosting, tuning, thresholds. Together:
   demo, decks, report, GitHub.

Q: What is left?
A: Deeper tuning, the full ablation write-up, the scoring demo, the final
   deck (Nov 22), the report (Dec 6), and the GitHub repo.

PART 3: DEFINITIONS
==================================================

Cross-entropy (log loss): The loss that penalizes confident wrong
  probabilities; minimized by logistic regression and gradient boosting
  here.
Entropy split criterion: A tree chooses splits that most reduce entropy
  (cross-entropy of the class mix); an alternative to Gini impurity.
Cross-validation: Repeatedly training on one part of the data and
  validating on another to estimate performance and pick settings.
Time-aware (expanding-window) cross-validation: Folds that always train on
  earlier data and validate on later data.
PCA (principal component analysis): Rotates the data into uncorrelated
  directions ordered by variance; keeping the first few reduces
  dimensions.
Explained variance: The share of the data's variation that a component
  carries.
t-SNE: A nonlinear method that places similar points near each other in
  2D; used for visualization.
PR-AUC: Area under the precision-recall curve; summarizes performance
  across all thresholds for rare-class problems.
Threshold: The score cutoff for flagging fraud, chosen from validation
  predictions.
Ablation: Re-training with groups of features removed to see which ones
  help.
Concept drift: When a pattern (here, the fraud rate) changes over time.

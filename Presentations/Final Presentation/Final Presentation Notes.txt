--- SLIDE 1: FRAUD DETECTION: FINAL RESULTS (Josh) ---
Hello everyone, I'm Josh, and this is Logan. This is our final
presentation on detecting credit card fraud with machine learning. We'll
cover our data and whether it is believable, how our history features
avoid leakage, our eight models, how we handle the class imbalance and
tune each model, and the final results. Logan will begin with the

--- SLIDE 2: THE DATA, AND IS IT BELIEVABLE? (Logan) ---
Thanks, Josh. Our goal is to flag fraudulent credit card transactions
from the amount, category, time, and a customer's own history. The
challenge is that fraud is only 1.1% of transactions, so we judge models
by F1 and PR-AUC, never accuracy alone. We generated 877,369
transactions for 998 customers across 2025 with the public Sparkov tool,
so no private data is involved, and built 31 model inputs in four
groups: base details, time, distance from home, and customer history.
Before trusting any result, we asked whether the simulated data is
believable, so we read how the Sparkov generator creates fraud. Every
customer gets one short burst of fraud, about a day long, drawn from a
separate fraud profile instead of the normal one. In that profile a
customer makes many more purchases, spends more on online shopping,
groceries and other online purchases, spends larger amounts, shops
mostly at night, and does not travel. We then checked that our generated
data matches that design. 984 of 998 customers have a fraud burst,
averaging 9.5 fraud transactions. The median fraud amount is 500 dollars
against 44 for normal purchases, and 86% of fraud happens at night
against 30% of normal purchases. Distance from home is the same for
both, which is why distance carried no signal. So the generator does
what it documents. What it cannot tell us is whether real fraud looks
like this. We can only say the patterns we see, large amounts, night
purchases and online categories, are ones commonly described for card
fraud, and that distance from home, the fraud rate and the one-burst
design are clearly simpler than real life, so absolute scores would
likely be lower on real data while the comparisons between methods are
the part to trust. The fraud rate is a setting, one burst per customer,
and real card fraud is typically far rarer than one percent, so we use
this data to compare methods fairly, not to predict real-world accuracy.
It also explains our drift: fraud per day stays flat all year, but
Sparkov roughly doubles normal volume in the holiday weeks, so the
December fraud rate falls to 0.5%. Josh will explain how the history
features work and why they cannot leak.

--- SLIDE 3: HISTORY FEATURES, AND WHY THEY CANNOT LEAK (Josh) ---
Here is what the history group in our features means, and why it cannot
leak. For every purchase we describe the customer's own past: how big
this purchase is compared with their typical amount so far, how many
purchases they made in the last hour and the last day, how long since
their last purchase, and how fast they would have had to travel. In this
example, a customer's earlier purchases average about 43 dollars, and
then an 890 dollar purchase arrives, so the amount is about twenty times
their normal. The rule is that a feature may only use purchases that
happened strictly before the one being scored. We enforce that in three
ways. First, our running averages are running totals minus the current
row, so a purchase never counts toward its own average. Second, the
counts for the last hour and day only look at earlier timestamps. Third,
the validation folds and the test month are in time order too, so no row
is ever scored with knowledge of its future. If we let the future in,
our scores would look better than any real system could achieve. Logan
will now cover the eight models.

--- SLIDE 4: EIGHT MODELS AND THEIR LOSS FUNCTIONS (Logan) ---
Thanks, Josh. We compared eight classical models, four from each of us.
Josh's are logistic regression, a decision tree, a random forest, and
extra trees. Mine are XGBoost, LightGBM, a linear support vector
machine, and Naive Bayes. Wherever a model has a loss function, it is
cross-entropy. Logistic regression, XGBoost, and LightGBM minimize log
loss directly, and the tree, forest, and extra trees choose splits by
entropy. The linear SVM trains on hinge loss, which is a margin loss, so
we calibrate its scores with log loss to get probabilities. Naive Bayes
has no loss: it is fit by maximum likelihood. Josh will explain how we
handle the class imbalance.

--- SLIDE 5: HANDLING THE CLASS IMBALANCE (Josh) ---
Fraud is about one in a hundred transactions, so a model that always
answers not fraud would be 98.9% accurate and catch nothing. We keep the
data imbalanced and make the model care about the rare cases, in four
steps. First, class weights: a missed fraud costs the model much more
than a false alarm. Logistic regression, the decision tree, the forest,
extra trees and the SVM use balanced class weights, which makes each
fraud example count about 84 times more. For XGBoost and LightGBM the
fraud weight is a setting we tuned, trying 1, 3, 9, 20, 40 for XGBoost
and 1, 3, 9 for LightGBM, and cross-validation picked 40 and 3. Naive
Bayes has no weighting. Second, we judge models by PR-AUC and F1, not
accuracy. Third, each model's fraud cutoff is picked in cross-validation
to maximize F1, rather than left at 0.5. Fourth, validation and test
data keep the real fraud rate, and we only change how the model trains.
One caveat: weighting makes scores good for ranking, but they are not
true probabilities. We did not try resampling such as SMOTE, which is
future work. Logan will explain how we tuned and validated them.

--- SLIDE 6: HYPERPARAMETER TUNING (Logan) ---
Thanks, Josh. We validated with time-aware cross-validation: the January
to November development period is cut into six chunks, and in each of
five folds the models train on the earlier chunks and validate on the
next one, so they never see the future. December is held out and scored
once at the end. We tuned every model's settings with grid search on
those same folds. A grid search tries every combination from a short
list of settings and keeps the one with the best average PR-AUC across
the five folds. Our first pass tried two to four combinations per model.
For the final version we widened the grids to between 4 and 150
combinations per model, adding the fraud weight, number of trees, and
sampling settings for the boosted models. Because the boosted models'
best values kept landing at the edge of the grid, we searched them in
three rounds, each finer grid centered on the previous winner. Cross-
validation chose every winner. The test month was never used for tuning.
The table shows what we searched, how many combinations we tried, and
what won. The biggest cross-validated gains were LightGBM from 0.46 to
0.64 and XGBoost (gradient boosting) from 0.51 to 0.69. Josh will
compare the results.

--- SLIDE 7: MODEL COMPARISON (Josh) ---
Thanks, Logan. Here are all eight models on the test month, next to
their cross-validated scores. The random forest has the best cross-
validated PR-AUC, 0.72 with a spread of 0.07, and on test it reaches a
PR-AUC of 0.76 and an F1 of 0.71. After tuning, the boosted models have
caught up: LightGBM has the highest test PR-AUC, 0.81, with an F1 of
0.73, and XGBoost reaches 0.75 and 0.69. We still call the forest our
best model because we chose by cross-validation and did not switch after

its validation folds swing from 0.06 to 0.79, so we trust it less. Both
linear models fail, with PR-AUC of 0.006 for logistic regression and
0.011 for the linear SVM, because fraud depends on combinations of
features that a straight line cannot express. Naive Bayes is stable but
weak at 0.15. Logan will explain what made the boosted models better. At
its tuned threshold the random forest caught 443 of 652 fraud cases with
158 false alarms among about 130,954 normal transactions.

--- SLIDE 8: WHAT MADE THE BOOSTED MODELS BETTER (Logan) ---
Thanks, Josh. Here is what we learned about XGBoost and LightGBM, which
we expected to do best. In our first pass they trailed the forest, with
cross-validated PR-AUC of 0.51 and 0.46 against 0.72. Tuning changed
that. XGBoost rose to 0.69 and LightGBM to 0.64 in cross-validation, and
on the test month to 0.75 and 0.81. What helped was making the boosted
models smaller and more regularized, not bigger: only 30 trees, each
built from half the rows and half the columns, with a heavier fraud
weight. On the validation folds, a bigger XGBoost with 400 trees and
depth 10 scored 0.32 against 0.69 for the tuned one, so extra capacity
overfits. We also tested whether city size and age, which identify

lifted the oversized model from 0.32 to 0.44, but barely changed the
tuned model, 0.69 to 0.68, so memorizing customers is only part of the
story. Our reading is that boosting builds trees one after another and
can chase noise in a small fraud class that drifts over time, while the
forest averages many independent trees. We did not prove that. The
forest still leads in cross-validation, 0.72 against 0.69 and 0.64. Josh
will show the precision-recall curves for all eight models.

--- SLIDE 9: PRECISION-RECALL CURVES FOR ALL EIGHT MODELS (Josh) ---
Thanks, Logan. This is the curve behind PR-AUC. For every possible
cutoff, it plots precision, the share of flagged transactions that are

right means catching more fraud, usually at the price of more false
alarms. PR-AUC is the area under each curve, so higher and further right

The random forest keeps its precision above 50 percent until about 81%
recall, and the decision tree is close behind. LightGBM and XGBoost

their near-zero PR-AUC. Logan will wrap up with our conclusions and who

--- SLIDE 10: CONCLUSIONS AND WHO DID WHAT (Logan) ---
Thanks, Josh. We have four main findings. First, the random forest was

PART 2: QUESTIONS YOU MAY GET, WITH ANSWERS
==================================================

Q: What loss function do you use?
A: Wherever a model has a loss, it is cross-entropy. Logistic regression
   minimizes binary cross-entropy (log loss). XGBoost and LightGBM use
   the binary logistic objective, which is also log loss. The decision
   tree, random forest, and extra trees choose splits with the entropy
   criterion, built from cross-entropy, instead of Gini. The linear SVM
   trains on hinge loss, which is a margin loss, so we then calibrate it
   with log loss (Platt scaling). Naive Bayes has no loss: it is fit by
   maximum likelihood.

Q: How did you use cross-validation?
A: Time-aware, expanding-window cross-validation with 5 folds over January
   to November. Each fold trains on earlier chunks and validates on the
   next chunk, so the models never train on the future. Settings were
   chosen by mean PR-AUC across folds, thresholds from the pooled
   validation predictions, and December was held out until the end. We
   also record F1 for every fold. Random forest averaged PR-AUC 0.72 +/-
   0.07 and F1 0.67.

Q: Why not ordinary shuffled k-fold?
A: Shuffling mixes future transactions into training and inflates scores,
   because history features and fraud patterns depend on time. Time-
   ordered folds match how a real system is used.

Q: Why report both F1 and PR-AUC?
A: F1 depends on a threshold, and PR-AUC does not. F1 shows how the model
   behaves at the cutoff we would actually use; PR-AUC compares the
   models' ranking of transactions independent of that cutoff. When they
   disagree, the gap tells us the threshold did not transfer. That
   happened to XGBoost and LightGBM in our first pass (F1 0.30 and
   0.19); after tuning it largely went away.

Q: What is SVD, and how is it different from PCA?
A: SVD rewrites the data table as new directions ordered by how much of the
   data they capture. PCA is SVD after centering each column. We scale
   columns but do not center them, so the first direction mostly
   reflects overall level. It takes 22 of 31 components to keep 95% of
   the energy, versus 25 for PCA.

Q: What did you find with dimensionality reduction?
A: SVD on all features collapses every model (forest 0.011 versus 0.76).
   SVD on only the 13 continuous features, keeping flags and categories,
   keeps most performance for the forest (0.72) and LightGBM (0.71) but
   hurts extra trees (0.26 versus 0.49). t-SNE shows fraud forms local
   clusters: 85% of a fraud point's 10 nearest neighbors are fraud.

Q: Why did SVD on all features hurt so much?
A: We observed it but did not isolate the cause. A likely reason is that
   the fraud signal lives in flags and categories (such as the night
   flag) that a rotation across all features mixes together. We treat
   that as a hypothesis.

Q: Which model is best?
A: By cross-validation, the random forest: PR-AUC 0.72 +/- 0.07. On the
   test month it has PR-AUC 0.76 and F1 0.71. After tuning, LightGBM
   (0.81), the decision tree (0.77) and XGBoost (0.75) are close on the
   test month. The tree's validation folds range from 0.06 to 0.79, so
   we trust it less.

Q: How much did tuning change XGBoost and LightGBM?
A: A lot. Cross-validated PR-AUC went from 0.51 to 0.69 for XGBoost and
   from 0.46 to 0.64 for LightGBM. On the test month, PR-AUC went from
   0.63 to 0.75 and from 0.73 to 0.81, and F1 from 0.30 to 0.69 and from
   0.19 to 0.73. In the first pass their thresholds (0.99 and 1.00) were
   too strict after the final refit, so they flagged few cases. The
   tuned models use smaller, more regularized trees and a different
   fraud weight, so their scores and thresholds transfer much better.

Q: LightGBM scores highest on the test month. Why isn't it your best model?
A: We choose the best model by cross-validation, where the random forest
   leads (0.72 versus 0.64). Switching after seeing the test month would
   turn the test month into a selection set, so it would no longer be an
   honest one-time check. The gaps are also small on a single month with
   652 fraud cases: LightGBM has PR-AUC 0.81, the tree 0.77, the forest
   0.76, and XGBoost 0.75. We report all of them and treat the top tree
   models as close.

Q: Why do logistic regression and the linear SVM fail?
A: Test PR-AUC is 0.006 and 0.011. Fraud depends on combinations of amount,
   hour, and customer history, which a straight-line boundary cannot
   express. The SVM's cross-validation is also unstable (0.40 +/- 0.29).
   They remain useful baselines.

Q: Why is Naive Bayes weak but stable?
A: Its cross-validated PR-AUC is 0.30 +/- 0.04, the smallest spread of any
   model, because it has almost nothing to tune. But it assumes features
   are independent, which they are not (amount and amount-versus-
   average, for example), so it is capped at a low score (0.15 on test).

Q: Why didn't distance from home help?
A: In this generated data, fraud is no farther from home than normal
   purchases (medians 49.1 vs 48.9 miles; single-feature ROC-AUC 0.50).
   It is a finding about the simulator, not about real fraud.

Q: How do you avoid leakage?
A: History features use only a customer's earlier transactions (running
   totals subtract the current row; counts look only at earlier
   timestamps), the split and folds are time-ordered, scalers and the
   SVD are fit on training data only, and December was evaluated once.

Q: Why does the test fraud rate differ?
A: Fraud is 1.2% of transactions in January to November but 0.5% in
   December. Fraud per day is flat through the year, but Sparkov raises
   normal volume in the holiday weeks (Nov 30 to Dec 31), so the
   December share falls. It is an artifact of the generator, and we list
   it as a limitation.

Q: How much fraud is in the data?
A: 9,377 of 877,369 transactions, 1.1%, across 998 customers. Always
   guessing 'not fraud' would be 98.9% accurate.

Q: Some transaction numbers repeat. Is the data duplicated?
A: No. No full row is repeated. The generator's seed makes some transaction
   ID strings repeat across different customers, and we never use that
   column as a feature.

Q: Is any of this real data?
A: No. It is simulated with the public Sparkov generator. No private or
   personal data is used.

Q: Is this deep learning?
A: No. All eight models are classical machine learning.

Q: Who did what?
A: Josh: logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, extra trees,
   plus the feature pipeline and ablation. Logan: XGBoost, LightGBM,
   calibrated linear SVM, Naive Bayes, plus tuning and thresholds for
   them. Together: demo, decks, report, GitHub.

Q: Explain the history features and how they prevent leakage (Intermediate slide 5).
A: History features describe a customer's own past: the amount compared
   with their typical amount so far, purchases in the last hour and day,
   time since the previous purchase, and implied travel speed. A feature
   may only use purchases strictly before the one being scored. Running
   totals are cumulative sums minus the current row, so a purchase never
   counts toward its own average. Counts look only at earlier
   timestamps, and a first purchase with no history is filled with zero
   (features.py). The split and the cross-validation folds are also in
   time order, and the scalers and SVD are fit on training data only.
   Leakage would make our scores better than any deployed system could
   achieve.

Q: How do you know the Sparkov data is good, especially the fraud versus normal proportions?
A: We read how Sparkov creates fraud and checked our data against it
   (validity.py). Every customer gets one short fraud burst drawn from a
   separate fraud profile: more purchases, a different category mix,
   larger amounts, night-time shopping, no travel. In our data 984 of
   998 customers have a burst of 9.5 purchases on average; the median
   fraud amount is $500 versus $44, and 86% of fraud happens at night
   versus 30% of normal purchases. So the generator does what it
   documents. That is an internal check: it does not prove real fraud
   looks like this. The 1.1% fraud share is a setting (one burst per
   customer), not a measurement, and real card datasets such as the
   ULB/Worldline one have about 0.17% fraud (add a reference). So we use
   the data to compare methods fairly, not to claim real-world accuracy.

Q: Why does the fraud proportion change between months?
A: Fraud per day is flat (about 26 a day) but normal volume per day jumps
   in December (4,141 versus about 2,280 in November) because Sparkov
   weights the holiday weeks more heavily. The December fraud share
   therefore falls to 0.5%. It is an artifact of the generator.

Q: How do you handle the class imbalance?
A: We keep the data imbalanced and make the models care about the rare
   class, in four ways. (1) Class weights: logistic regression, the
   tree, forest, extra trees and the SVM use balanced class weights, so
   a fraud example counts about 84 times more; XGBoost and LightGBM use
   scale_pos_weight, a tuned setting (XGBoost: 1, 3, 9, 20, 40;
   LightGBM: 1, 3, 9); Naive Bayes has no weighting. (2) PR-AUC and F1,
   not accuracy, because always saying not fraud is 98.9% accurate. (3)
   A cutoff tuned in cross-validation to maximize F1, not the default
   0.5. (4) Validation and test data keep the real fraud rate; only
   training changes. We did not use SMOTE or undersampling. Weighted
   scores rank well but are not true probabilities.

Q: Does class_weight='balanced' make the data balanced?
A: No. The data stays 99% normal. 'balanced' is the scikit-learn name for
   weighting each class inversely to how common it is, so a fraud
   example counts about 84 times more in the loss. The forest and extra
   trees use 'balanced_subsample', which recomputes the weights inside
   each tree's sample.

Q: What does the precision-recall curve show?
A: For every possible cutoff it plots precision (the share of flagged
   transactions that are fraud) against recall (the share of all fraud
   we catch). PR-AUC is the area under the curve. The flat gray baseline
   is a random guess, whose precision is the test fraud rate (0.5%). The
   random forest keeps precision above 50% until about 81% recall;
   logistic regression and the linear SVM sit close to the random-guess
   line.

Q: What is grid search, and how did you tune hyperparameters?
A: Grid search tries every combination from a short list of settings and
   keeps the best one. We wrote our own loop (cv.py) so each combination
   is scored on the same time-aware folds, and the winner has the best
   mean PR-AUC. The grids (common.py) have 4 to 150 combinations per
   model; the first pass had two to four. The boosted models got a
   second, finer grid around the first winner, because its best values
   sat at the edge of the first grid. The test month is never used for
   tuning.

Q: Why not scikit-learn's GridSearchCV?
A: We could have used it with TimeSeriesSplit. A custom loop gave us the
   pooled out-of-fold predictions we need to choose each model's
   threshold, per-fold PR-AUC and F1, and a disk cache so an interrupted
   run resumes.

Q: Shouldn't XGBoost be better than the random forest?
A: After tuning it is close. In our first pass XGBoost trailed (cross-
   validated PR-AUC 0.51); after a wider grid searched in three rounds
   it reaches 0.69 in cross-validation and 0.75 on the test month,
   against 0.72 and 0.76 for the forest. What helped was making it
   smaller and more regularized: 30 trees, half the rows and columns
   each, and a fraud weight of 40. On the validation folds a bigger
   XGBoost (400 trees, depth 10) scored 0.32 against 0.69 for the tuned
   one, so more capacity overfits. Dropping city size and age, which
   identify customers, moved the bigger model from 0.32 to 0.44 but the
   tuned model only from 0.69 to 0.68, so memorizing customers is only
   part of the story. Our reading, not proven: the forest averages many
   independent deep trees, which suits a small fraud class that drifts
   over time, while boosting fits trees one after another and can chase
   noise. XGBoost often wins on real tabular data; this is simulated
   data.

Q: Is this binary classification or probability?
A: Binary classification: the label is fraud or not. Each model outputs a
   score between 0 and 1. PR-AUC uses the scores across all cutoffs, and
   F1 uses one tuned cutoff. Because of class weights the scores rank
   well but are not calibrated probabilities (only the SVM is explicitly
   calibrated).

Q: Did you use PCA?
A: No. We used SVD (scaled, not centered). PCA appears only as a
   comparison: PCA is SVD after centering, and it needs 25 components
   for 95% of the energy versus 22.

Q: What drives the predictions?
A: For the random forest, shuffling amount hurts most, then the night flag,
   hour of day, and amount compared with the customer's average.
   Distance from home is not a leading feature.

Q: Do the features help?
A: Yes, the history ones. Random forest PR-AUC is 0.61 with base, time, and
   category only, 0.77 when history features are added, and 0.61 when
   distance is added to the base set instead.

Q: What would you do with more time?
A: Try real data with permission, cost-based thresholds, wider tuning
   grids, and generator settings where travel matters.


==================================================
PART 3: DEFINITIONS
==================================================

Hyperparameter: A setting chosen before training, such as tree depth or
  the number of trees. It is not learned from the data.
Grid search: Trying every combination from a short list of
  hyperparameter values and keeping the one with the best validation
  score. Ours is scored on time-aware folds.
Precision-recall curve: For every cutoff, precision (share of flagged
  transactions that are fraud) plotted against recall (share of all
  fraud caught). PR-AUC is the area under it.
Class imbalance: One class (fraud, about 1%) is far rarer than the
  other. We keep the data as is and use class weights, PR-AUC and F1,
  and a tuned cutoff.
class_weight balanced / scale_pos_weight: Settings that make a fraud
  example count more in the loss. 'balanced' weights each class
  inversely to its frequency; scale_pos_weight sets the fraud weight
  directly. They do not change the data.
SMOTE: A resampling method that invents synthetic examples of the rare
  class. We did not use it; it is future work.
Binary classification: Predicting one of two labels (fraud or not). Our
  models output a score between 0 and 1, and a cutoff turns it into a
  label.
Cross-entropy (log loss): The loss that penalizes confident wrong
  probabilities. Logistic regression, XGBoost, and LightGBM minimize it
  directly.
Entropy split criterion: A tree chooses the split that most reduces
  entropy (the cross-entropy of the class mix), instead of the default
  Gini impurity. Used by our tree, forest, and extra trees.
Hinge loss: A margin loss used by support vector machines. It is not
  cross-entropy, so we calibrate the SVM's scores with log loss
  afterward.
Probability calibration (Platt scaling): Fitting a small logistic curve
  that turns a model's raw scores into probabilities, by minimizing log
  loss.
Maximum likelihood: Choosing parameters that make the training data most
  probable. Naive Bayes is fit this way and has no separate loss
  function.
Logistic regression: A model that learns one weight per feature and
  outputs a probability.
Decision tree: A flowchart of yes/no questions that ends in a
  prediction.
Random forest: Many decision trees, each trained on a random slice of
  the data, that vote.
Extra trees (extremely randomized trees): Like a random forest, but
  split points are chosen more randomly, which adds variety between
  trees.
Gradient boosting / XGBoost: Trees built one after another, each
  correcting the previous ones' mistakes. XGBoost is a popular, fast
  implementation.
LightGBM: Another boosting implementation. It grows trees leaf by leaf,
  which is fast but can overfit with large trees, so we keep ours small.
Linear SVM (support vector machine): Finds the straight-line boundary
  with the widest margin between the classes. Fast, but it cannot
  express non-linear patterns.
Naive Bayes: Applies Bayes' rule while assuming features are
  independent. Very fast and stable, but the independence assumption is
  wrong here.
SVD (singular value decomposition): Rewrites a data table as new
  directions ordered by how much of the data they capture. We keep the
  first few to reduce dimensions.
PCA (principal component analysis): SVD applied after subtracting each
  column's mean (centering). We use uncentered SVD, so the first
  direction mostly reflects overall level.
Energy (explained variance): The share of the data that a component
  captures. For uncentered SVD it is the squared singular value divided
  by the sum of all of them.
t-SNE: A nonlinear method that places similar points near each other in
  2D, used only for visualization.
Cross-validation: Repeatedly training on one part of the data and
  validating on another to estimate performance and choose settings.
Time-aware (expanding-window) cross-validation: Folds that always train
  on earlier data and validate on later data, so a model never sees the
  future.
F1 score: One number balancing precision and recall at a chosen
  threshold.
PR-AUC: Area under the precision-recall curve. It summarizes performance
  across all thresholds and suits rare-class problems.
Precision / recall: Precision: of the transactions we flag, how many are
  fraud. Recall: of all fraud, how much we catch.
Threshold: The score cutoff for flagging fraud, chosen from the pooled
  validation predictions.
Class weights: A multiplier that makes mistakes on the rare class
  (fraud) count more during training.
Ablation: Re-training with groups of features removed or added to see
  which ones help.
Concept drift: When a pattern, here the fraud rate, changes over time.
Data leakage: When a model gets information it would not have in real
  life, such as future transactions.

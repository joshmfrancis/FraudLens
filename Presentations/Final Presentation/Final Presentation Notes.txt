Hello everyone, I'm Josh, and this is Logan. This is our final

--- SLIDE 2: PROBLEM AND GOAL (Logan) ---
Thanks, Josh. Our goal is to flag fraudulent credit card transactions from
the amount, category, time, and a customer's own history. The challenge is
that fraud is rare: 1.2% of our 822,484 simulated transactions, or roughly
one in a hundred, as in this picture. A model therefore has to find a few
needles in a large haystack, and plain accuracy would be misleading. Josh
will describe the data and features.

--- SLIDE 3: DATA AND FEATURES (Josh) ---
We generated 822,484 transactions for 999 customers across 2025 with the
public Sparkov tool, so no private data is involved. From the raw columns
we built 31 model inputs in four groups: base details, time, distance from
home, and customer history. The history features, such as the amount
compared with a customer's average, use only that customer's earlier
purchases, so nothing leaks from the future. Logan will show how we
reduced the dimensionality of these inputs.

--- SLIDE 4: DIMENSIONALITY REDUCTION: PCA (Logan) ---
Our 31 inputs overlap a lot, so we applied principal component analysis,
or PCA, which rotates the data into new directions ordered by how much
variation they carry. It takes 25 of the 31 components to keep 95 percent
of the variance, and the first two keep 17 percent. The first component
mostly reflects recent activity, such as purchases in the last day, and
the second mostly reflects amount. In the two-dimensional view, fraud
overlaps normal activity heavily, so no simple projection separates it.
Josh will show a nonlinear view and what happens when models use the
reduced inputs.

--- SLIDE 5: T-SNE AND PCA IN THE MODELS (Josh) ---
To look for structure that PCA can't show, we used t-SNE, a nonlinear
method, on a balanced sample of 3,000 fraud and 3,000 normal transactions.
Fraud forms local clusters: on average 84 percent of a fraud point's ten
nearest neighbors are also fraud. We then trained the models on reduced
inputs. Applying PCA to only the 13 continuous features keeps most of the
performance, with the random forest at 0.70 versus 0.76. Applying PCA to
all 31 features collapses it to 0.00, because the fraud signal sits in
flags and categories that the rotation scrambles. We did not test that
explanation separately. Logan will explain how we validated the models.

--- SLIDE 6: METHODS AND CROSS-VALIDATION (Logan) ---
We used time-aware cross-validation. The January to November development
period is cut into six chunks, and in each of five folds the models train
on the earlier chunks and validate on the next one, so they never see the
future. We picked each model's settings by average PR-AUC across the
folds, and chose its decision threshold from the pooled validation
predictions. Then each model was refit on the whole development period and
tested once on December. All four models optimize cross-entropy: logistic
regression and gradient boosting minimize log loss directly, and the tree
and forest split on entropy. The folds also show how unstable some models
are: the random forest averages 0.73 with a spread of 0.10, while gradient
boosting's spread is 0.19. Josh will compare the results.

--- SLIDE 7: MODEL COMPARISON (Josh) ---
Here are all four models on the test month, next to their cross-validated
scores. The random forest is best on both: a test PR-AUC of 0.76, an F1 of
0.72, and a cross-validated PR-AUC of 0.73 with a spread of 0.10. The
decision tree reaches 0.69, and gradient boosting 0.66, although its F1 is
only 0.30 because the threshold chosen in cross-validation was too strict
for the refit model, so PR-AUC is the fairer comparison. Logistic
regression fails, with a PR-AUC of 0.006, because fraud depends on
combinations of features that a straight-line model cannot express. Logan
will look closer at the best model.

--- SLIDE 8: BEST MODEL IN DETAIL (Logan) ---
On the test month, the random forest caught 373 of 571 fraud cases and
raised 85 false alarms among about 122,802 normal transactions. That's a
precision of 0.81 and recall of 0.65 at a threshold of 0.88. The threshold
matters a lot: at the default of 0.5, its F1 would drop to 0.36. The curve
shows the trade-off between catching more fraud and raising more false
alarms. Josh will explain what drives these predictions.

--- SLIDE 9: WHAT DRIVES FRAUD (Josh) ---
This chart shows which features the random forest relies on most, measured
by how much performance drops when we shuffle each one. Amount is the
clear leader, followed by the night flag and the hour of day. Amount
compared to the customer's own average also ranks high, which is one of
our history features. Distance from home does not appear, which matches
what we saw in the data. Logan will test whether the feature groups really
help.

--- SLIDE 10: DO OUR FEATURES HELP (Logan) ---
We tested this with an ablation, retraining the random forest on different
feature groups and scoring each on the test month. A base model with
amount, time, and category scores a PR-AUC of 0.57. Adding the history
features lifts it to 0.77, a large gain. Adding distance does not help:
0.52. Removing distance and speed from the full set changes nothing
meaningful, 0.76 versus 0.76. So the history features earned their place,
while distance did not in this data. Josh will show the demo.

--- SLIDE 11: LIVE DEMO (Josh) ---
We built a small web app that scores a transaction with all four models. A
coffee near home scores 12 out of 100 and looks normal. An 890 dollar
online purchase at two thirty in the morning scores 98 and is flagged as
likely fraud, driven mostly by amount and time of day. A large evening
purchase near home scores 69, which the app labels worth a look. The app
also shows what moved each score. I will now run it live. Logan will cover
the limitations.

--- SLIDE 12: LIMITATIONS AND LESSONS (Logan) ---
We want to be clear about the limits and lessons. The data is simulated,
so results may not transfer to real spending. Distance from home carried
no signal in this generator. Fraud also drifts over time: 1.3% of January
to November transactions were fraud, but only 0.5% in the test month, and
cross-validation scores varied across folds. The main lessons were that
accuracy is a trap, that thresholds do not always transfer, and that
features must use only the past. Josh will summarize the conclusions.

--- SLIDE 13: CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK (Josh) ---
We have three main findings. First, the random forest was our best model,
with a test F1 of 0.72, a test PR-AUC of 0.76, and the most stable cross-
validation scores. Second, customer-history features mattered most,
lifting PR-AUC from 0.57 to 0.77. Third, dimensionality reduction helped
us understand the data but not the models: PCA on the continuous features
kept most of the performance, and PCA on everything did not. Distance from
home, our original idea, did not help in this simulated data. For future
work we would try real data with permission, cost-based thresholds, and
generator settings where travel matters. Logan will close.

PART 2: QUESTIONS YOU MAY GET, WITH ANSWERS
==================================================

Q: What loss function do you use?
A: Every model optimizes cross-entropy. Logistic regression minimizes
   binary cross-entropy (log loss). Gradient boosting uses XGBoost's
   binary:logistic objective, which is also log loss. The decision tree
   and random forest choose splits with the entropy criterion (information
   gain, which is built from cross-entropy), instead of the default Gini
   impurity.

Q: How did you use cross-validation?
A: Time-aware, expanding-window cross-validation with 5 folds over January
   to November. Each fold trains on earlier chunks and validates on the
   next chunk, so the models never train on the future. Settings were
   chosen by mean PR-AUC across folds, and December was held out until the
   end. Random forest averaged 0.73 ± 0.10.

Q: Why not ordinary shuffled k-fold?
A: Shuffling mixes future transactions into training and inflates scores,
   because history features and fraud patterns depend on time. Time-
   ordered folds match how a real system is used.

Q: What dimensionality reduction did you do?
A: PCA on the 31 standardized inputs (it takes 25 components to keep 95%
   of the variance), a t-SNE view of a balanced sample, and models trained
   on reduced inputs. PCA on only the 13 continuous features kept most
   performance (random forest 0.70 vs 0.76); PCA on all features collapsed
   it (0.004).

Q: Why did PCA on all features hurt so much?
A: We observed it but did not isolate the cause. A likely reason is that
   the fraud signal lives in flags and categories (such as the night flag)
   that a rotation across all features mixes together. We treat that as a
   hypothesis.

Q: What does t-SNE show?
A: On a balanced sample of 3,000 fraud and 3,000 normal transactions,
   fraud forms local clusters: 84% of a fraud point's 10 nearest neighbors
   are also fraud. t-SNE is for visualization only, so we did not train on
   it.

Q: Which model is best?
A: The random forest: test PR-AUC 0.76, F1 0.72, and the most stable
   cross-validation (0.73 ± 0.10).

Q: How did you pick the threshold?
A: From the pooled validation predictions in cross-validation, choosing
   the cutoff that maximizes F1. For the random forest it is 0.88; at the
   default 0.5, its test F1 would be 0.36.

Q: Why is gradient boosting's F1 so low?
A: Its PR-AUC is 0.66, but the decision threshold chosen in cross-
   validation (0.99) was too strict after the final refit, so it flags
   only 99 fraud cases (F1 0.30). PR-AUC does not depend on a threshold,
   so it is the fairer comparison.

Q: Why is logistic regression so weak?
A: PR-AUC 0.006. Fraud depends on combinations of amount, hour, and
   customer history, which a straight-line model cannot express. It
   remains a useful baseline.

Q: Why didn't distance from home help?
A: In this generated data, fraud is no farther from home than normal
   purchases (medians 49.0 vs 48.9 miles; single-feature score 0.50). It
   is a finding about the simulator, not about real fraud.

Q: How do you avoid leakage?
A: History features use only a customer's earlier transactions, folds are
   time-ordered, and December was evaluated once.

Q: Why does the test fraud rate differ?
A: Fraud is 1.3% of transactions in January to November but 0.5% in
   December. The generator does not spread fraud evenly across months. We
   list it as a limitation.

Q: Is any of this real data?
A: No. It is simulated with the public Sparkov generator. No private or
   personal data is used.

Q: Who did what?
A: Josh: logistic regression, decision tree, feature pipeline, ablation.
   Logan: random forest, gradient boosting, tuning, thresholds. Together:
   demo, decks, report, GitHub.

Q: How does the demo score a transaction?
A: The server builds the same features used in training, runs all four
   saved models, and reports each score. 'What moved the score' replaces
   one feature group with a neutral value and shows how much the score
   changes. Scores are model outputs, not calibrated probabilities.

Q: What would you do with more time?
A: Real data with permission, cost-based thresholds, SMOTE, and generator
   settings where travel matters.

PART 3: DEFINITIONS
==================================================

Cross-entropy (log loss): The loss that penalizes confident wrong
  probabilities; minimized by logistic regression and gradient boosting
  here.
Entropy split criterion: A tree chooses splits that most reduce entropy
  (cross-entropy of the class mix); an alternative to Gini impurity.
Cross-validation: Repeatedly training on one part of the data and
  validating on another to estimate performance and pick settings.
Time-aware (expanding-window) cross-validation: Folds that always train on
  earlier data and validate on later data.
PCA (principal component analysis): Rotates the data into uncorrelated
  directions ordered by variance; keeping the first few reduces
  dimensions.
Explained variance: The share of the data's variation that a component
  carries.
t-SNE: A nonlinear method that places similar points near each other in
  2D; used for visualization.
PR-AUC: Area under the precision-recall curve; summarizes performance
  across all thresholds for rare-class problems.
Threshold: The score cutoff for flagging fraud, chosen from validation
  predictions.
Ablation: Re-training with groups of features removed to see which ones
  help.
Concept drift: When a pattern (here, the fraud rate) changes over time.

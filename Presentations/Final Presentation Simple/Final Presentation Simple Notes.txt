Slide 2 (LOGAN)
Thanks, Josh. Credit card fraud is rare: only about 1.1% of transactions, roughly one in a hundred. That makes it hard, because a model that always says not fraud is 98.9% accurate and catches nothing. So we judge models by PR-AUC and F1, not accuracy. Real card data is private, so we generated ours with Sparkov, a public simulator: 877,369 transactions for 998 customers across 2025. We checked that the data follows the generator's own rules, but simulated fraud is simpler than real fraud, so we trust the comparisons between methods more than the exact scores. Josh will explain what we built.

Slide 3 (JOSH)
We compared eight classical models, four each, with no deep learning: logistic regression, a decision tree, a random forest and extra trees from me, and XGBoost, LightGBM, a linear support vector machine and Naive Bayes from Logan. Three choices matter most. First, history features: for every purchase we compare it with that customer's own earlier purchases, using only the past, so nothing leaks from the future. Second, time-ordered testing: models always train on earlier months and are tested on later ones, and December was held out and scored once. Third, we keep the data imbalanced but make the models care about the rare fraud cases, using class weights, a tuned cutoff, and PR-AUC and F1 instead of accuracy. Logan will show how we tuned the models.

Slide 4 (LOGAN)
Thanks, Josh. We tuned each model with grid search: try many combinations of settings and keep the one that scores best across time-ordered validation folds. The test month was never used for tuning. This mattered most for the boosted models. XGBoost went from a cross-validated PR-AUC of 0.51 to 0.69 and LightGBM from 0.46 to 0.64, and on the test month XGBoost's F1 went from 0.30 to 0.69 and LightGBM's from 0.19 to 0.73. What helped was making them smaller and giving fraud more weight, not bigger. The other models changed little. Josh will show the final results.
